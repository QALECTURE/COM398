01 / Start with a question

Your phone disappears. What have you actually lost?

Classroom scenario · 5 minutes

You leave your phone on a café table. Two minutes later, it is gone.

Ask a partner: What could happen next? Does losing the device automatically mean losing access to all your data?

Possible consequences
  • Loss of a physical device and potentially valuable information.
  • Exposure of accounts if the thief can bypass authentication.
  • Loss of access to authentication codes or recovery methods.
  • Potential exposure of university or workplace information.

But: device encryption, screen locks and account security can reduce the damage. A stolen phone does not automatically expose everything.

Today's big idea: security protects information, identity, services and devices.

03 / Core security concepts

Three things every secure system needs

C / Confidentiality

Who can see it?

Only authorised people should access sensitive information.

Banking example: Your account balance should not be visible to strangers.

I / Integrity

Can we trust it?

Information should not be changed without authorisation.

Banking example: A £100 transfer must not become £1,000.

A / Availability

Can we use it?

Systems should remain accessible when needed.

Banking example: You need the service to work when making a payment.

Threat, vulnerability, attack and risk

Vulnerability

The unlocked door

A weakness: an application allows unsafe database queries.

Threat

The potential intruder

A person or event could exploit the weakness.

Attack

The break-in

Someone attempts to exploit the weakness, for example with malicious input.

Risk

What might happen?

Consider the likelihood and impact of the threat exploiting the weakness.

Think-pair-share: A university portal goes offline during an exam. Which part of the CIA triad is most directly affected?

Availability. If grades are also altered without permission, integrity is affected.

How does a secure connection work?

HTTPS is HTTP carried over a TLS-secured connection. TLS helps protect data in transit and authenticate the server for the domain you visit, when certificates are correctly validated. It does not prove that a website's operator is honest. A phishing site can also use HTTPS.

Quick question: Is a padlock in the browser enough to prove a website is legitimate? No. Check the domain and context, not just the padlock.

05 / Security beyond the classroom

Four technologies worth knowing about

Contemporary context for discussion. These are established developments, not a live news feed.

Identity

Passkeys

Passkeys use public-key cryptography and are designed to resist phishing by binding sign-in to the correct service.

Ask: Why might this be safer than reusing a password?

Infrastructure

Cloud security

Cloud providers and customers have different security responsibilities. Misconfigured access can still expose information.

Ask: Who controls permissions on a company's cloud storage?

Cryptography

Post-quantum security

NIST finalised its first three post-quantum cryptography standards in 2024: FIPS 203, 204 and 205.

Ask: Why plan a cryptographic migration before a threat arrives?

Emerging practice

AI and security

AI can assist defenders with analysis and can also help attackers produce convincing phishing messages.

Ask: Can you always recognise a phishing message by poor spelling?

Further reading: UK NCSC · NIST · OWASP

07 / Tutorial 1 · Hands-on

Binary: how computers represent numbers

Decimal is base 10 and uses digits 0–9. Binary is base 2 and uses only 0 and 1. Each position is worth twice the position to its right. A bit is one binary digit; a byte is eight bits.

Predict the bits
